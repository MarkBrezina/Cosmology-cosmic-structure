The Expanding Voids Test

You proposed that empty space grows in proportion to how much of it there already is, and that this growth shapes both how galaxies spin and how fast the universe expands. This page writes the idea as equations and checks it against 175 galaxies and 1,508 supernovae.

The idea under test

Every cubic metre of empty space grows by a small fixed fraction of itself. Across the large empty stretches in a galaxy's sparse outskirts this growth adds up and changes how fast the outer stars move. Between galaxies, the expanding voids press inward on them, so the local Hubble rate differs from place to place while averaging to the global value.

Holds

The growth rule itself

Growth in proportion to the space already there is exactly how a cosmological constant behaves. Supernovae fix the rate: a cubic metre of empty space gains 0.18 mm³ a year.

Section 1
Fails

Faster outer stars

Expanding space pushes outward, and at the measured rate the push is about 14,000 times too weak at a galaxy's edge. Given any strength at all, it bends rotation curves upward where real ones stay flat.

Sections 2 and 3
Holds, already explained

A Hubble rate that varies

Individual galaxies scatter by ±22% around the global rate at 20 Mpc, shrinking to a few per cent by 100 Mpc. Gravity from the mapped distribution of galaxies already predicts this pattern.

Section 5

A lead worth following. Galaxies start needing extra pull below an acceleration of 1.14 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s². The speed of light times the cosmic expansion rate, divided by 2π, gives the same number. Your sense that galaxy outskirts and cosmic expansion are connected has real support there. Section 4 shows what that connection would have to look like.

Your rule, written as physics

If every volume V of empty space grows at a rate proportional to itself, then dV/dt = αV. The solution is exponential growth, so volumes grow as eαt and distances as eαt/3. In general relativity this is precisely how empty space behaves when it carries a cosmological constant, Λ, which is the leading description of dark energy. Your rule and the standard model's dark energy are the same idea.

dV/dt = α V  ⇒  V ∝ eαt,   distances ∝ eαt/3
α = 3HΛ,   HΛ = H0√ΩΛ = √(Λc²/3) the expansion rate empty space would keep on its own
ρvac = α² / (24πG) the energy density that rate implies

We fitted the Pantheon+ supernova distances (1,657 supernovae with the full error covariance and the SH0ES Cepheid calibration) with a flat universe made of matter plus your expanding vacuum. The fit gives H₀ = 73.5 ± 1.0 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ and a vacuum share ΩΛ = 0.668 ± 0.018, which pins down your growth constant.

Is the rate really constant?

A constant α corresponds to an equation of state w = −1. The same supernovae give w = −0.91 ± 0.15, consistent with a constant. Combining galaxy-clustering data from DESI with the cosmic microwave background and supernovae hints that dark energy is slowly weakening, which in your language would mean α is drifting down (section 6).

2

Inside a galaxy, expanding space pushes the wrong way

Stars in the outskirts of spiral galaxies orbit much faster than the visible matter can explain. Holding a star on a faster orbit takes more inward pull. In general relativity a cosmological constant adds a small outward acceleration, a = HΛ²r, that grows with distance from the centre. It makes outer stars orbit slightly slower.

The direction matters for the original intuition too. Expansion acts along the line to the centre, while the rotation speed astronomers measure runs along the orbit. Expansion can only change the speed by changing the balance of forces, and it changes that balance the wrong way.

Then there is the size. At the outermost measured point of each galaxy we compared the outward push with the extra inward pull the observations require. Across 169 galaxies the push is about 14,000 times smaller (median). Adding it to the visible matter changes the overall fit by 0.03%.

The amount of empty space barely changes inside a galaxy

Stars fill about one part in 10²³ of the volume near the Sun and about one part in 10¹⁷ in the crowded galactic centre. By volume, the centre and the edge are both essentially all empty space. What falls with distance from the centre is the density of matter, so a version of the idea that can work has to depend on density, or on something density controls.

3

Giving the idea every advantage

Suppose instead that the voids around a galaxy squeeze it, so the push points inward, and let its strength be anything at all, separately for each galaxy. The extra acceleration is then a = H²r with H free. This is the most generous version of the idea that still has its basic shape: an effect that grows with the amount of space, and so with radius.

It needs a rate about 130 times the cosmic one

Fitted galaxy by galaxy, the push needs a median H of 9,500 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ (the middle two thirds of galaxies need 5,300 to 14,200). The universe as a whole expands at 73.5. If space inside galaxies really expanded that fast, a galaxy's size would double in about 70 million years.

It gets the shape wrong

A push that grows with radius makes a rotation curve keep rising, like a spinning plate. Real curves flatten. Averaged over all 171 galaxies, even the best-fitting push leaves the model about 20% too slow between a fifth and two fifths of the way out, and 22% too fast at the edge.

The same test can be run without any fitting. Take the acceleration the visible matter fails to supply and see how it changes with radius across the outer half of each galaxy. Your rule says it should grow in proportion to radius, a slope of +1 on a log-log plot. It falls with radius in 91% of galaxies, with a median slope of −0.64. Only 2% come within reach of +1.

It is not one property of space

A property of empty space should take the same value everywhere. Instead, each galaxy needs its own value, spread over a factor of 23, and the rate a galaxy needs falls as its mass rises (Spearman ρ = −0.54 across 115 galaxies). Forcing one shared value on all galaxies gives H = 7,700 ± 400 and doubles the scatter around the data compared with the acceleration law in section 4.

What the best-fitting push really is

An inward push that grows in proportion to radius is exactly the pull of a uniform fog of mass. The median best fit equals about 200,000 proton masses per cubic metre (3.4 × 10⁻²² kg/m³) spread evenly through the galaxy, about half the dark-matter density estimated near the Sun. Real dark-matter haloes look uniform only in their cores, which is why the push fits slowly rising dwarfs such as IC 2574 and fails wherever curves go flat.

4

What the galaxies actually demand

Your instinct that low density matters turns out to be right, once low density means a weak pull from the visible matter. Plot the mass discrepancy (the acceleration we observe divided by the one visible matter provides) against radius, and galaxies scatter widely. Plot it against the visible matter's own acceleration and they collapse onto one curve.

Scatter around the trend drops from 0.27 dex against radius to 0.15 dex against acceleration. A single number describes all 164 galaxies: the discrepancy switches on below a₀ = (1.14 ± 0.07) × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s², with 0.13 dex of intrinsic scatter. This is the radial acceleration relation found by McGaugh, Lelli and Schombert in 2016, reproduced here with the same data.

A second regularity says the same thing from another angle. The speed at which a galaxy's rotation curve flattens is set by its visible mass alone, across four decades in mass.

The acceleration where galaxies start needing extra pull matches the speed of light times the cosmic expansion rate divided by 2π. Nobody knows yet whether this is a deep connection or a coincidence. It is the strongest hint that galaxy outskirts and cosmic expansion are linked, which is your intuition.

The match also shows what a working version needs. Anything built only from nearby space expanding at a rate H produces accelerations of order H²r. The observed scale is cH₀. The ratio of the two is c/(H₀r), the Hubble radius of the universe divided by the size of the galaxy, about 136,000 at 30 kpc. A successful version has to let each galaxy respond to the cosmic horizon, not only to the empty space beside it. Milgrom's modified dynamics (MOND) and Verlinde's emergent gravity are two attempts in exactly this direction.

5

Between galaxies, the Hubble rate really does vary

Divide each supernova host galaxy's recession speed by its distance and you get that galaxy's own Hubble rate. Nearby, these scatter widely. Farther out they converge on one value, as your idea predicts.

Figures 9 and 10 use:

The spread is ±22% at 15–30 Mpc, ±14% at 30–50 Mpc, ±9% at 50–80 Mpc and ±7% at 80–130 Mpc, where supernova distance errors of about 6% take over. Shell averages beyond 40 Mpc sit within 3% of the global value. One simple rule explains the shape of the funnel: each galaxy carries a random velocity of about 334 ± 21 km/s on top of the expansion, and a fixed velocity matters less the farther away the galaxy is.

The variation you predicted is real, and ordinary gravity from the visible distribution of galaxies predicts its pattern. Underdense regions expand a little faster because there is less matter slowing them down, and overdense regions expand more slowly. Velocities predicted from the 2M++ map of galaxies (Carrick et al. 2015) account for most of the coherent flow, and leave room for an extra push from voids of at most about 20% of gravity's effect (2σ).

Voids do push in one real sense. The Milky Way's motion is partly set by the Dipole Repeller, a large underdense region that drives us away about as strongly as the Shapley concentration pulls (Hoffman et al. 2017). In standard cosmology that push is the absence of gravity: matter drains off voids toward walls and filaments.

Expansion also wins outright at large enough separations. For the Local Group (about 2 × 10¹² solar masses) the vacuum's outward push equals the group's gravity at 1.3 Mpc. Inside that distance gravity rules, and beyond it expansion takes over. Your intuition holds between galaxy groups, at megaparsec scales, and fails inside galaxies, which are about a hundred times smaller.

Is our whole neighbourhood a void? Comparing supernovae inside 300 Mpc with those beyond, the local expansion comes out faster by 0.3 ± 0.7% using redshifts as observed, and by 1.1 ± 0.7% after subtracting the motions gravity predicts. The local-void explanation of the Hubble tension involves a void about 20% underdense out to roughly 300 Mpc and is still debated; neither split shows a step that large, though the corrected one is a mild hint rather than nothing.

Where the research stands, October 2026

  • Dark energy may be weakening

    DESI's three-year galaxy map, combined with the cosmic microwave background and supernovae, prefers dark energy that changes with time, at 2.8 to 4.2σ depending on the datasets. In your terms, α may be slowly falling. DESI finished its planned five-year survey in April 2026.

    University of Waterloo on the DESI results · DESI announcements

  • Timescape: voids expanding faster as the whole explanation

    David Wiltshire's timescape model says clocks run faster in voids, so voids have expanded more, and the growing void fraction mimics acceleration without dark energy. A 2024 analysis of Pantheon+ claimed very strong evidence for it over the standard model. It still has to match the baryon acoustic oscillation, lensing and microwave-background data. This is the published idea closest to yours.

    Royal Astronomical Society summary of Seifert et al. (2024)

  • The Hubble tension has not gone away

    The H0 Distance Network's 2026 consensus puts the local rate at 73.50 ± 0.81 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, against about 67.2 predicted from the microwave background. The TDCOSMO lensed-quasar result (71.1 +2.4/−2.3) stays in tension with Planck even without supernovae.

    ScienceDaily on the H0DN result · TDCOSMO 2026

  • Are we inside a giant void?

    A local underdensity of about 20% out to 300 Mpc would make nearby expansion look faster. Banik and Kalaitzidis (2025) found that 20 years of baryon acoustic oscillation measurements fit such a void better than a void-free model. A void that large and deep fits poorly with the standard model, so the idea remains controversial.

    Banik & Kalaitzidis, MNRAS 540 (2025)

  • Testing gravity at low acceleration

    Wide binary stars orbit with accelerations near a₀, so they test whether gravity itself changes there. A 2026 study with strict quality controls found Newtonian gravity up to 1,500 times more likely than MOND, while Chae (2025) reports an anomaly. The question is open.

    Wide-binary quality framework (2026) · Chae, ApJ 985 (2025)

  • Emergent gravity

    Erik Verlinde (2016) derived an apparent dark-matter effect from the dark energy of an expanding universe, with a switch-on acceleration of about cH₀/6. The first weak-lensing test agreed with it; rotation-curve tests have been mixed. It is the clearest worked example of the horizon-scale link section 4 calls for.

    Verlinde (2016) · Brouwer et al. (2017), first lensing test
7

What would make the idea work

Each test above turns into a requirement. A version of the expanding-space idea that explains galaxy rotation would need all of these.

  1. Pull outer stars inward.An outward push slows orbits.
  2. Fade as 1/r beyond the visible disc.That keeps rotation curves flat. A push proportional to r makes them climb.
  3. Scale with each galaxy's visible mass, so that mass ∝ speed⁴.The amount of empty space around a dwarf and a giant is the same; their outer speeds differ tenfold.
  4. Switch on below about 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s², the value of cH₀/2π.The galaxy has to respond to the cosmic horizon, not only to nearby space.
  5. Stay invisible where accelerations are high.Planetary orbits show no such effect, and the most careful 2026 wide-binary analysis sees none either.
  6. Do dark matter's other jobs.Gravitational lensing, galaxy clusters such as the Bullet Cluster, the pattern of peaks in the microwave background, and the growth of large-scale structure.
  7. Behave like a near-constant α ≈ 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ per year in cosmology today.Possibly drifting down slowly, if the DESI hint holds.

Tests you can run next with the analysis script

  • Make the push depend on the visible matter's acceleration, gextra = f(gvisible, cH₀), and fit the function f. That is the road from your idea to the acceleration law.
  • Split galaxies by environment using a void catalogue. If expanding voids squeeze galaxies, void galaxies should rotate differently from wall galaxies of the same mass.
  • Let stellar mass-to-light ratios float per galaxy and check whether any version of the push survives that extra freedom.

Methods and data

How each number on this page was produced
  • Rotation curves. SPARC (Lelli, McGaugh & Schombert 2016, AJ 152, 157): 175 galaxies, 3,391 points, with mass models for gas, stellar disc and bulge. Stellar mass-to-light ratios fixed at 0.5 (disc) and 0.7 (bulge) at 3.6 µm. The files came from a public GitHub copy of the SPARC release, because the SPARC site was not reachable from the analysis machine; distances match the published database.
  • Models. Visible matter only; plus the vacuum push −HΛ²r at the measured rate; plus an inward push +H²r with one shared H or a free H per galaxy; the acceleration law g = gbar / (1 − e−√(gbar/a₀)) with one shared a₀; a pseudo-isothermal dark-matter halo with two free numbers per galaxy. Fits use the published velocity errors. Per-galaxy comparisons use 171 galaxies with at least five points.
  • Acceleration relation. Points with velocity errors under 10% and positive visible-matter acceleration: 2,803 points in 164 galaxies, fitted in log space with free intrinsic scatter; a₀ uncertainty from 150 bootstrap resamples of galaxies. The original 2016 analysis also removed low-quality and nearly face-on galaxies, which needs a table that was not available here.
  • Mass–speed relation. The 118-galaxy table of Lelli, McGaugh & Schombert (2016, ApJL 816, L14), fitted with errors in both variables.
  • Supernovae. Pantheon+SH0ES (Scolnic et al. 2022; Brout et al. 2022) with the full statistical plus systematic covariance. The cosmology fit uses Cepheid distances for the calibrator galaxies and supernovae above z = 0.01, fitting H₀, Ωm and the supernova absolute magnitude together. Planck's H₀ of 67.4 would lower α to 1.71 × 10⁻¹⁰ per year.
  • Local Hubble rates. Each supernova's rate compares its measured distance with the distance its redshift implies, using CMB-frame redshifts as observed or redshifts corrected with the 2M++ peculiar-velocity model supplied with Pantheon+. Scatter is split by maximum likelihood into supernova noise (0.103 mag intrinsic, plus measurement error) and random velocities. Dipole and regression use 450 supernovae at 0.01 < z < 0.06.
  • Expected variation in standard cosmology. Linear theory gives δH/H = −(f/3) δ with growth rate f = Ωm0.55 = 0.55; a BBKS power spectrum with σ₈ = 0.81 predicts typical shell-to-shell differences of about 6% at 30 Mpc, 1.6% at 100 Mpc and 0.3% at 330 Mpc.
Data: SPARC (Lelli, McGaugh & Schombert 2016), Pantheon+SH0ES (Scolnic et al. 2022; Brout et al. 2022), 2M++ velocity model (Carrick et al. 2015). Analysis script: expanding_voids_analysis.py. All fits on this page were computed on 26 October 2026.
